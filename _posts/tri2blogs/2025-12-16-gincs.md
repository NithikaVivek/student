### Designing With Humans in Mind: Reflections from a Girls in CS Panel

Attending the Girls in CS panel reshaped the way I think about computer science—not as a purely technical discipline, but as one rooted in people. Across different academic paths and career stages, every panelist emphasized a shared principle: good technology begins and ends with human needs.

One panelist described how AP classes built her foundation in critical thinking and writing—skills that, surprisingly, became just as important as coding itself. Writing clear documentation, breaking down large goals into smaller subgoals, and setting realistic timelines are not just academic exercises; they’re essential for building software that others can understand, use, and maintain. This idea came up repeatedly when panelists talked about wireframing, UI design, and structuring APIs within frameworks that can scale and even auto-generate components. These processes force developers to think not just about what they are building, but who they are building it for.

What struck me most was the emphasis on human–computer interaction (HCI). Several panelists discussed how software engineering is not about having total creative freedom at all times—especially in internships, where you’re often told what to build. Yet within those constraints lies the real challenge: interpreting user needs, synchronizing data across systems (often with the help of LLMs), and iterating based on customer research and reviews. One panelist explained how consulting-style projects pushed her to propose solutions for different companies, using machine learning not just for novelty, but to identify meaningful patterns that could drive impact.

The conversation around limitations was especially refreshing. Rather than viewing them as roadblocks, panelists framed limitations as design constraints—opportunities to think more deeply. Whether it was learning to recover from a project that went wrong, navigating ambiguous requirements, or being “thrown into the deep end” of college-level STEM classes, each experience reinforced resilience. Many panelists started CS later than their peers and openly admitted they didn’t know what they were doing at first. Instead of hiding that uncertainty, they leaned into curiosity—and grew because of it.

Impact beyond the screen also mattered. One panelist spoke about founding a nonprofit focused on teaching coding through online and in-person events, webinars, and workshops. This idea of giving back to the community highlighted a broader view of CS: technology as a tool for empowerment. Others echoed this sentiment through involvement in school ambassador programs, collaboration on large-scale projects, and leadership in team environments.

Collaboration, in particular, emerged as a critical skill. Writing meaningful commit messages, leaving clear comments, and being able to verbally explain code were described as just as important as writing the code itself. One insight stayed with me: when you work independently, you have a clear vision—but that vision doesn’t automatically translate to others. Bridging that gap requires communication. Panelists shared strategies like learning concepts individually, documenting them clearly, and aligning teams through shared documents, weekly Scrum meetings, and agile sprints.

High school rigor also played a role. Academically challenging STEM classes taught time management, discipline, and how to perform under pressure—skills that translated directly into public speaking, teamwork, and meeting deliverable deadlines. These experiences prepared the panelists not just for college, but for real-world engineering environments.

Ultimately, the panel reinforced why I’m drawn to computer science: the intersection between humans and machines. The most meaningful projects weren’t the most technically complex—they were the ones that focused on users, impact, and collaboration. Technology isn’t just about building apps; it’s about understanding people, anticipating their needs, and designing systems that genuinely improve their experiences.

Leaving the panel, I felt more confident that CS isn’t about having everything figured out early. It’s about staying curious, being resilient, seeking out mentors, networking intentionally, and continuously shaping your own learning path. Most importantly, it’s about remembering that behind every line of code is a human on the other side of the screen.
